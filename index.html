04 / 28   System Mapping · Macroview
▼

The Medicines-Use System — a Macroview

Before we zoom into electronic prescribing, we must zoom out. EP is not a product; it is one node inside a sociotechnical network of people, paper, software, regulations, and pharmacology.

Figure 1 — Macroview of the medicines-use system. The hospital (left, closed loop) and community (right, open loop) are linked by an eTP/API bridge that today is partial in most countries, including Jordan.

Reading the map: four lessons in one picture

  1. Hospital EP is a closed loop — every actor (prescriber, pharmacist, nurse, lab) shares one EHR and one patient identifier (the MRN on the wristband). Decision support has rich data to draw from: weight, eGFR, allergies, current INR.
  2. Community EP is an open loop — the prescription is portable (the patient can take it to any pharmacy), and the data shared between GP, pharmacy, and insurer is far thinner. The CDS engine knows much less.
  3. The bridge is the weakest link — discharge prescriptions must cross from the hospital EP system to the GP EMR and ultimately the community pharmacy. In most countries (and certainly in Jordan), this is still partly manual.
  4. EP changes the visibility of risk — paper hides errors; electronic systems surface them. This is why error rates can appear to rise after go-live before they fall.
Why this matters

If you only ever see EP as a screen with drug names on it, you will miss the system. The exam — and your future hospital director's office — will reward students who can see the whole map: people, data flows, regulators, and the gaps between them.

In Jordan, the macroview has three real anchors: Hakeem (the public-sector EHR rolled out by Electronic Health Solutions across MoH hospitals, RMS, and university hospitals), private hospital EHRs (Cerner, InterSystems TrakCare, and locally built systems at Istishari, Khalidi, Specialty), and community pharmacy software (varied, mostly dispensing-focused, rarely connected to Hakeem). The bridge between hospital discharge and community pharmacy is overwhelmingly paper — a printed prescription handed to the patient at discharge.

05 / 28   Why we needed EP
▼

The pre-digital pain points

Before electronic prescribing, the medicines-use cycle was a sequence of handovers built on paper, memory, and bleeped phone calls. Each handover was a place where errors hid.

1

Illegible handwriting

The classic image of a doctor's scrawl is not a joke — it is a documented patient-safety hazard. Pharmacy callbacks to the ward to clarify "Inderal vs Inderide" or "Losec vs Lasix" were daily events.

2

Incomplete prescriptions

Missing route, missing frequency, missing duration, missing signature, missing date. Optional paper fields invite omission. Pharmacists then guess — or chase the prescriber.

3

Transcription errors

In jurisdictions where unit clerks re-typed orders into the pharmacy system (US-style), ~11% of all prescribing errors were transcription errors. EP eliminates this entire category overnight.

4

Lost or duplicate drug charts

Paper drug charts at the bedside got soaked, shredded, lost, or — worse — duplicated across shifts. A second chart with a slightly different regimen is a never-event waiting to happen.

5

Slow ward-to-pharmacy comms

A new prescription written at 9 a.m. could take 3+ hours to reach pharmacy via the porter system. First doses of antibiotics were delayed; sepsis bundles missed their golden hour.

6

No audit trail

Who prescribed? Who changed the dose? Who administered? When? On paper, the answer is "ask the team" — and memory is fallible. There is no defensible record for litigation, governance, or quality improvement.

Real example

In the UK National Reporting and Learning System (NRLS), medication errors are the second-largest category of incidents reported by NHS hospitals. Every prescribing error reported on paper was a missed opportunity that EP — properly designed — could have caught at the point of entry.

Walk through the medical ward of a Jordanian public hospital today and you will still see paper drug charts at the foot of the bed in many sites. Hakeem-enabled hospitals have moved to electronic charts, but legacy paper persists in surgical wards, ICU during downtime, and crisis surge periods. The lessons of pre-EP pain points are not history — they are this morning's reality for many of your colleagues.

06 / 28   Definitions
▼

What is Electronic Prescribing?

The utilisation of electronic systems to facilitate and enhance the communication of a prescription or medicine order, aiding the choice, administration and supply of a medicine through knowledge and decision support, and providing a robust audit trail for the entire medicines use process.

— NHS Connecting for Health

Three capabilities baked into the definition

A

Communication

Electronic transmission of the order between prescriber, pharmacy, nurse, and patient — replacing handwriting, faxing, and paper drug charts.

B

Knowledge & Decision Support

Active alerts (allergy, interactions, dose range) and passive references (BNF, formulary) embedded in the prescribing workflow.

C

Audit Trail

A timestamped, attributable, immutable record of every order, every override, every administration. The legal and quality-improvement substrate.

Examiner's lens

If a colleague says "we have electronic prescribing" but the system only captures orders without alerts or audit, they have CPOE, not EP. The definition is your weapon: ask "does it support knowledge?" and "does it audit?" If both answers are no, it is not EP.

07 / 28   Terminology
▼

EP vs CPOE vs eTP — clearing the confusion

These three terms are routinely treated as synonyms in casual hospital conversation. They are not. The differences shape what your system can safely do.

Term Scope Setting Key features
Electronic Prescribing (EP) Full medicines cycle — prescribing, supply, administration Hospitals (UK term) Decision support, administration tracking, audit trail
Computerized Physician Order Entry (CPOE) Data capture only — narrower US hospitals (legacy term) Order entry without integrated decision support; applies to any order (labs, imaging, drugs)
Electronic Transfer of Prescriptions (eTP) Transmission only — sending the script across organisations Community / primary care Sends prescriptions from GP to community pharmacy electronically (e.g., NHS EPS, US SureScripts)
e-Prescribing (generic) Umbrella term used loosely in policy documents Any setting Beware: the same word covers all three of the above in different contexts

Why this matters in practice

📋 Clinical implications

When hospital staff describe their system as "electronic prescribing", they may assume it includes decision support — but CPOE alone does not. This mismatch causes:

  • Overconfidence in system safety
  • Failure to implement compensating safeguards (like pharmacist screening)
  • Misunderstanding of training needs

📊 Research implications

Studies that compare "EP systems" may actually be comparing very different functionalities. A CPOE without CDS will reduce errors very differently from full EP. Clear definitions are essential for:

  • Comparable studies and meta-analyses
  • Sensible vendor procurement specifications
  • Realistic implementation business cases

In Jordan, vendor pitches and tender specifications often use "EP", "CPOE", and "e-prescribing" interchangeably. As a future pharmacy administrator, your first job in any procurement is to demand the vendor define exactly what their module does — and which of the three columns above it actually fills.

08 / 28   Anatomy of EP
▼

Core components of an EP system

Six modules, each of which can succeed or fail independently. A weakness in any one of them undermines the whole system.

Figure 2 — The six pillars of an EP system. The central core orchestrates flows; each pillar can fail independently.

📚

Medicines Database

Structured drug data with enough granularity to handle complex hospital regimens — not just "amoxicillin 500 mg PO TDS" but reducing-dose regimens, alternate-day schedules, dual-route (IV→PO switch), and infusion rates.

🖥️

User Interface

Screens and workflows for prescribing, reviewing, and administering. Tall-man lettering, drug images, structured fields, smart defaults — these UI choices determine error rates.

🛡️

Decision Support

Allergy checks, drug-drug and drug-disease interaction screening, dose validation, formulary guidance. We will spend a whole section on this.

🔌

Integration Layer

Interfaces (often HL7 v2 or FHIR APIs) to pharmacy stock systems, EHR demographics, pathology, discharge modules, and — critically — community systems for hand-off.

💉

Administration Module (eMAR + BCMA)

Records who gave what, when, and how. Barcode scanning at point-of-care verifies the 5 Rights. Without this module, EP is "half a system".

📊

Reporting & Audit Trail

Every prescribing event, edit, override, and administration is logged. Enables clinical governance, error rate tracking, and pharmaco-epidemiology research.

Architectural insight

You will see vendors sell "EP" that is really three of these six pillars in a trench coat. Use this map as your RFP checklist — does the proposed system have all six? Which are weak? What is the integration story for the missing pieces?

09 / 28   Drivers
▼

Why hospitals adopt EP — the four drivers

No CFO signs a multi-million-dinar EP contract because of pretty screens. There are four classic business cases.

🛡️

Patient Safety

Medication errors cause preventable harm and avoidable readmissions. EP provides structured interventions — alerts, dose checks, audit — that paper cannot. The safety case is the most quoted, but rarely the most decisive financially.

⚖️

Litigation & Risk Management

Healthcare is increasingly litigious. EP creates defensible documentation: who prescribed, when, and on what evidence. In a malpractice claim, the audit trail is the hospital's first witness.

🏛️

Clinical Governance

Transparent prescribing records support audit, antimicrobial stewardship, formulary compliance, and quality improvement. Regulators (CBAHI, JCI, MoH) increasingly require these capabilities for accreditation.

⚙️

Operational Efficiency

Electronic workflows streamline pharmacy dispensing (–64% turnaround), discharge prescriptions (80 days → same day), and supply-chain ordering. The CFO's case lives here.

In Jordan, the dominant drivers vary by sector. Public hospitals (MoH, RMS) emphasise governance and JCI/CBAHI accreditation. Private hospitals (Istishari, Khalidi, Specialty) emphasise operational efficiency and patient experience. University hospitals (JUH, KAUH) emphasise research-grade data and pharmaco-epidemiology. Knowing which driver dominates in your institution shapes the business case you would write.

10 / 28   Promises & limits
▼

What EP promises — and what it cannot guarantee

✅ Core promises

  • Legible, complete prescriptions
  • Reduced medication errors
  • Decision support at the point of care
  • Comprehensive, attributable audit trails
  • Streamlined ward-pharmacy workflows
  • Discharge summaries delivered on the day

🌟 Extended benefits

  • Formulary compliance and cost transparency
  • Supply-chain integration
  • Real-time KPI monitoring
  • Data for research, audit, and AMS

⚠️ Critical reality check

EP systems are tools, not magic. They cannot eliminate medication errors entirely. Poor design introduces new errors. Success depends on implementation, training, culture, and continuous monitoring.

  • EP ≠ error elimination — studies show 17–86% reduction depending on error type and system
  • Design determines benefit — a poorly designed system can increase errors
  • Human factors persist — workarounds, alert fatigue, cognitive load
  • Initial error rates may rise — better detection, learning curve
Sociotechnical lens

The single most important sentence in this lecture: "EP is a sociotechnical system, not a software product." Buying the software is 20% of the problem; designing the workflows, training the people, and shaping the culture is the other 80%.

11 / 28   Evidence · Hospital EP
▼

Headline evidence — error reduction

The literature is rich but heterogeneous. Three landmark studies anchor most lectures and exam questions.

1️⃣ Bates et al. (US, Brigham & Women's Hospital)

Figure 3 — Error reductions reported by Bates et al. across major error categories. Note the sharp contrast between potential errors (very high reduction) and preventable ADEs (modest reduction).

Critical reading

The 84% reduction in potential errors is dramatic — but a potential error is one caught before reaching the patient. The clinically meaningful figure is the 17% reduction in preventable ADEs. Always read the denominator and the outcome carefully. Headlines mislead.

2️⃣ Fowlie et al. (UK, Ayrshire & Arran)

SettingPre-EP error ratePost-EP (12 months)Significance
Inpatient prescribing7.4%4.7%p < 0.001 ✅
Discharge prescribing7.5%5.9%Not significant ⚠️
Medicines administration9.0%5.4%p < 0.001 ✅

Discharge prescribing already had a structured paper template, leaving little room for EP-driven improvement. Context determines impact.

In a Jordanian context, the closest analogue is the contrast between highly structured ICU drug charts (already templated, less room for EP gain) versus medical ward charts with dozens of free-text additions per shift (huge room for gain). Always think: where is the paper most chaotic? That is where EP delivers the biggest safety dividend.

12 / 28   The J-curve
▼

Why error rates often rise after go-live

A near-universal pattern: errors spike for 3–6 months, then decline below baseline. Understanding why protects you from premature panic.

Figure 4 — The classic J-curve: errors rise after go-live, peak in the first quarter, then fall below the pre-EP baseline as the system and people mature.

Three explanations

1

Enhanced detection

Electronic systems capture errors that paper charts hid. The errors did not increase — the visibility did. This is mostly good news, even if it looks bad on the dashboard.

2

Learning curve

Staff unfamiliar with new screens, dropdowns, and workflows make selection mistakes. This is real but transient — typically resolves over 3–6 months with good training.

3

System design flaws

If the curve does not recover, you have a design problem: confusing dropdowns, look-alike defaults, missing fields. Persistent post-go-live error rises are a red flag for governance.

For administrators

Build a 30-day, 90-day, and 6-month review into your EP rollout governance plan. Distinguish detection effects from real increases by sampling pre-EP charts retrospectively and applying the EP error definitions to them. Otherwise you are comparing apples (paper-era detection) with oranges (EP-era detection).

13 / 28   Iatrogenic errors
▼

The new errors EP creates

Koppel et al. (2005) identified 22 distinct error types facilitated by a commercial EP system after seven years of use. They fell into two families.

🧩 Family A — Information fragmentation

  • Allergies on one screen, prescription on another
  • Discontinued meds still listed in active orders
  • Critical labs (eGFR, INR) not visible at point of prescribing
  • Free text in one field contradicting structured data in another

🖱️ Family B — Human-machine interface

  • Dropdowns selecting the wrong drug (look-alike names)
  • Default doses accepted without adjustment
  • Easier to prescribe than to discontinue → polypharmacy
  • Click-fatigue overrides on important alerts

Concrete examples to remember

A

hydrALAzine vs hydrOXYzine

Two unrelated drugs whose names share the first six letters. In an alphabetised dropdown, they sit one above the other. Tall-man lettering (capitalised distinguishing letters) is the antidote.

B

Default dose acceptance

Adult default 240 mg gentamicin pre-populates for an 18-kg paediatric patient. Junior doctor clicks "OK" without adjusting. The dose is now 10× the safe dose. (See Case Study 2 in the cases module.)

C

Discontinuation friction

Ordering a new drug takes 4 clicks. Discontinuing an old one takes 8. Patients accumulate medications across admissions; polypharmacy creeps; deprescribing fails by design.

D

Free-text discrepancies

Magrabi et al. studied 2,914 prescriptions with free text. 16.1% had internal discrepancies — structured field said "10 mg" but free text said "two tablets". 83.8% led to adverse events. The fix: minimise free text.

Mental model

EP eliminates whole categories of error (illegibility, transcription) and creates new ones (selection, default acceptance, fragmentation). Net safety only improves if the new errors are fewer, less frequent, and less severe than the old ones — which requires continuous post-implementation surveillance.

14 / 28   Paediatrics
▼

Paediatric EP — why the stakes are higher

Paediatric prescribing is high-risk by nature: weight-based dosing, narrow therapeutic indices, age-related pharmacokinetics, and rampant off-label use. EP can help — but only if it is designed for paediatric complexity.

⚖️

Weight-based dosing

Doses calculated mg/kg or mg/m². Adult-default systems are catastrophic when applied to a 15 kg child.

📐

Narrow therapeutic index

Aminoglycosides, anticonvulsants, chemotherapy — small dose deviations cause large harm.

🧬

Age-related PK/PD

Neonates, infants, children, and adolescents are not "small adults". Renal/hepatic clearance varies dramatically.

Evidence highlights

StudyFindingTake-home
Ghaleb et al.EP reduces dosing errors and missing information in paediatrics, but adoption is slowVendors under-invest in paediatric modules
Jani et al.Error rate fell from 77.4% → 4.8% in renal paediatric patientsMostly omission errors prevented by structured prompts
Warwick et al.Prescribing errors fell from 8.8% → 4.6% at 6 months; omitted doses reduced significantlySustained gain when system supports paediatric workflows

Jordan's tertiary paediatric care concentrates at King Hussein Cancer Center (KHCC) paediatric oncology, Queen Rania Children's Hospital, and the paediatric units at JUH and KAUH. Any EP system serving these sites must include weight-/BSA-based dosing calculators, paediatric formulary, age-banded high-dose alerts, and dilution rules for neonatal IV preparations. A generic adult-focused EP module is unsafe in these contexts.

Lesson

If your hospital has a paediatric unit and your EP system does not have a dedicated paediatric module, that is a clinical risk you must register on the corporate risk register. "We bought EP" is not the same as "we made paediatric prescribing safer".

15 / 28   BCMA · The 5 Rights
▼

Barcode Medicines Administration (BCMA)

EP without BCMA is a half-loop. The closing of the loop happens at the bedside, where the nurse, the medication, and the patient meet.

Figure 5 — BCMA enforces the "5 Rights" at the bedside by scanning patient and medication, verifying against eMAR, and logging administration in real time.

Evidence on effectiveness

−74%Target adverse events (Poon et al., 369k events)
−63%All adverse events (Poon)
82.6 → 18.9%Non-definitive patient ID (Charing Cross, UK)
+ ComplianceMore scanning → more error reduction
Definition refresher

The Five Rights of medication administration: Right Patient · Right Drug · Right Dose · Right Route · Right Time. Some institutions add Right Documentation and Right Reason for a "Seven Rights" framework. BCMA mechanises the first five.

16 / 28   BCMA workarounds
▼

BCMA workarounds — and why they happen

Koppel et al. identified 15 distinct BCMA workarounds across five hospitals. Workarounds are not a moral failing of nurses — they are a sign that the system, the workflow, or the culture is misaligned.

⚙️

Technical failures

Scanners broken or unavailable. Wireless black spots. System lag. The first time the scanner fails, the nurse improvises. The tenth time, improvisation becomes the norm.

⏱️

Time pressure

Scanning every dose for 15 patients on a 2-hour med round adds 20 minutes. On a short-staffed night shift, that 20 minutes feels impossible.

🆕

Lack of awareness

Agency staff, locums, bank nurses, new starters — anyone outside the routine training cycle may simply not know the BCMA process or its rationale.

👥

Workaround culture

The most dangerous category. If experienced nurses say "we don't really scan on this ward", the workaround becomes invisible to managers and hospital-wide.

Common specific workarounds — and their risks

  • Batch scanning — scanning all meds for all patients at the start of shift. Risk: wrong drug or wrong patient at the bedside is no longer caught.
  • Wristband detached — scanning the loose wristband on the bedside table, not on the patient. Risk: the wrong patient receives the dose if beds were swapped.
  • Override "ID not confirmed" — bypassing the patient ID check with a verbal confirmation. Risk: defeats the entire purpose of BCMA.
  • Ward-stocking pre-labelled doses — labelling unit-dose packets ahead of time without scanning at the bedside. Risk: drift between physical pack and label.

How to manage — McNulty et al.

🤝

Foster ownership

Nurses must see BCMA as supporting their work, not policing it. Engage them in design, training, and post-rollout review.

🔧

Fix infrastructure

Address known weaknesses: black spots, broken scanners, slow networks. Workarounds rooted in tech failures cannot be trained away.

🚧

Hard stops with caution

Linking BCMA to automated dispensing cabinets so meds are inaccessible without scan can enforce compliance — but unintended consequences are real (Strom et al.).

Audit method

The single most powerful BCMA governance tool is the weekly scan-rate dashboard by ward and shift. Wards with low scan rates need a conversation, not a memo. The ward sister knows why scanning is hard — ask her, and you will find the fix.

17 / 28   BCMA limits
▼

BCMA — known limitations

BCMA is powerful but not free. Its limits define the scope of complementary controls you must put in place.

Hardware dependency

Scanners must be available, durable, charged, and compatible with the wireless infrastructure. A 10% scanner outage rate quickly becomes a 30% workaround rate.

Barcode availability

Not all medications carry usable barcodes. Repackaged items (compounded oncology, paediatric suspensions) require hospital-applied labels — adding cost and a new error pathway at the labelling step.

Standards harmonisation

Different barcode standards (EAN-13, GS1 DataMatrix, NDC) coexist. Interoperability issues affect smaller hospitals dependent on multiple suppliers.

Technology evolution

RFID, NFC, and computer vision (camera-based pack recognition) may eventually replace 1D barcodes. Investment cycles must anticipate this.

Original-pack dispensing required

BCMA works best when manufacturer packaging reaches the bedside. This conflicts with traditional ward pharmacy practices that decant into smaller containers.

Liquid and IV preparations

Compounded IVs, paediatric suspensions, and patient-specific TPN bags need pharmacy-printed barcodes that survive the supply chain.

For Jordanian hospitals planning BCMA, the practical first audit is: what fraction of doses leaving your central pharmacy carry a usable, scannable barcode? If the answer is below 70%, BCMA cannot be your primary safety net — it has to be paired with double-checks and pharmacist ward review.

18 / 28   Decision Support
▼

Clinical Decision Support — Active vs Passive

CDS is what turns EP from a typewriter into a clinical-intelligence partner. There are two flavours, both essential, neither sufficient alone.

DimensionActive CDSPassive CDS
DefinitionAutomatic alerts during workflow without the user seeking informationReference sources accessible via links when the user actively seeks information
ExamplesAllergy alerts, drug-drug interactions, dose-range checks, duplicate therapy warningsBNF online, UpToDate, Lexicomp, local formulary, hot-key links
IntegrationBuilt into the EP software — interruptiveStand-alone resources on intranet or external sites
StrengthCatches errors the prescriber might miss; enforces baseline safetyProvides depth for complex decisions without interruption
WeaknessRisk of alert fatigue if poorly configuredRelies on the user recognising when they need information; easily ignored

What good active CDS does well

🚫

Allergy checking

Cross-reference patient allergies against prescribed medications; flag sensitivities before harm occurs.

🧪

Drug-drug interactions

Identify pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic interactions; prioritise by severity (graded interactions).

♻️

Duplicate therapy

Detect two drugs from the same class (e.g., two NSAIDs) or the same drug under different brand names.

📏

Dose validation

Check doses against maximum recommended limits; adjust for renal/hepatic function; flag high-risk dosing.

📋

Formulary guidance

Steer prescribers toward formulary-approved options; display cost; promote generic prescribing.

🔬

Monitoring prompts

Remind clinicians to order necessary tests (INR for warfarin, digoxin levels, K+ for ACEi).

Design heuristic

Active CDS catches; passive CDS deepens. Both are needed. A system that only does active alerts becomes a noisy nag; a system that only does passive references becomes a dictionary the busy doctor never opens.

19 / 28   Alert fatigue
▼

Warning fatigue — the silent killer of CDS

97% of allergy alerts were overridden.

— Huntemann et al., on a real EP system in routine operation

If clinicians dismiss 97 out of 100 alerts, the system has lost credibility. The 3 alerts that mattered are buried under 97 that did not. The next time a real alert fires, it goes the same way as the rest: click, override, continue.

Causes of warning fatigue

📊

Low specificity

Alerts fire for clinically insignificant interactions (e.g., antacid reducing absorption of azithromycin) at the same level as catastrophic ones (clarithromycin + simvastatin → rhabdomyolysis).

📛

Poor prioritisation

Minor warnings presented with the same red font, the same modal pop-up, the same override-justification field as severe ones. The brain learns to ignore.

🔁

Interruptive design

Every alert blocks the workflow and demands an action. With 30+ alerts per shift, the prescriber's only survival strategy is reflexive override.

🧬

No specialty tuning

An oncology resident receives the same alerts as a paediatrician, even though their drug exposures are radically different. Untuned rules destroy specificity.

The fix — graded alerts

Figure 6 — Graded alerts: reserve the strongest interruption for the highest harm. Most alerts should never interrupt the prescriber.

Governance KPI

Track override rates per alert type. Any alert overridden > 80% of the time is not contributing to safety — it is contributing to fatigue. Either tune the rule, lower its severity tier, or retire it.

20 / 28   Hard stops
▼

Hard stops vs clinical judgment

A hard stop prevents a prescription from being entered at all — the system disallows the order. Should any prescription be absolutely forbidden? The literature gives a nuanced answer.

👍 The case for hard stops

Some prescriptions are unequivocally dangerous — "never events" that should never happen.

  • Intrathecal vincristine
  • Daily oral methotrexate for rheumatoid arthritis (should be weekly)
  • Adult-dose paracetamol for a neonate
  • QEHB Birmingham disallows orders for documented serious sensitivities

When the harm is catastrophic and irreversible, the system should refuse.

👎 The case against hard stops

Strom et al. studied a hard stop for warfarin + co-trimoxazole. The alert was effective — but caused clinically significant treatment delays.

  • Delays in necessary treatment
  • Frustrate clinicians who know the context
  • Drive workarounds (handwritten scripts, defeating EP)
  • Trial was terminated early due to harm from delay

Reserve hard stops for absolute contraindications. Use softer alerts (override-with-reason) for relative ones.

Decision rule

Use a hard stop only when both of these are true: (1) the harm is catastrophic and irreversible, and (2) there is no plausible legitimate clinical scenario in which this prescription is correct. If either is uncertain, use an interruptive alert with override-justification — not a hard stop.

Figure 7 — The 2×2 trade-off matrix. Aim for the upper-right quadrant: high safety and high usability.

21 / 28   Workflow design
▼

Why workflow design matters more than the screen

The best EP system with comprehensive CDS will fail if the workflow is clunky, unintuitive, or misaligned with clinical reality. Workflow determines whether clinicians embrace or subvert the system.

RiskCauseSolution
Incomplete prescriptionsOptional fields allow prescribers to skip critical detailsForced prompts; require all fields before signing
Incorrect drug selectionLook-alike names in dropdowns; ambiguous labelsTall-man lettering; separate similar drugs; pack images
Excessive steps12-stage prescribing process burdens busy cliniciansPre-Defined Orders (PDOs) for common prescriptions

Free-text discrepanciesFree text conflicts with structured dataMinimise free text; structured fields with validation
Long login timesStringent security forces re-auth every 5 minutesSmartcard tap-and-go; sensible session timeouts

Administration workflow — getting it right

⏰

Time windows

System defines when an order is "due" (e.g., ±1 hour of scheduled time) and when administration can be recorded. Too narrow → late doses; too wide → off-schedule drift.

🚫

Missed-dose codes

Structured reasons for non-administration (patient refused, drug unavailable, NPO for procedure, clinical decision). Free-text "not given" is useless to audit.

🔒

PRN lockouts

Prevent paracetamol administration more frequently than q4h or > 4 g/24 h. Safety embedded into workflow, not relying on nurse vigilance alone.

⏸️

On-hold / off-hold

Suspend prescriptions pending results (hold warfarin until INR available). Useful for elective treatments — chemotherapy, anticoagulants, electrolyte correction.

Design principle

Every field that is optional will be skipped by someone, in some shift. Every workflow that takes more than 90 seconds for a routine task will breed workarounds. Design for the worst day, not the demo day.

22 / 28   Integration · EHR
▼

EP and the EHR — integration as a multiplier

EP systems work best when integrated with the hospital's electronic health record (EHR). Stand-alone EP is functionally inferior; integration unlocks the system's intelligence.

What integration enables

📋 Comprehensive prescribing history

Prior admissions, outpatient prescriptions, GP records — all visible at the point of new prescribing. Reduces duplication and drug-disease conflicts.

🧪 CDS using patient data

Allergy alerts use the EHR allergy list. Renal-dosing alerts use the latest eGFR. Drug-disease checks use the problem list. Disconnected EP cannot do any of this safely.

🔔 Automatic monitoring orders

Prescribing warfarin auto-orders an INR. Prescribing vancomycin auto-prompts for trough levels. Closed-loop monitoring reduces preventable harm.

📡 Posting of lab results

Lab results post back into EP and trigger alerts: "K+ now 5.8 mmol/L — review ramipril". Real-time clinical surveillance.

Interoperability — the standards stack

HL7 v2 / v3Health Level 7 messaging — the legacy backbone for hospital integrations

SNOMED CTClinical terminology — coded concepts for diagnoses, procedures, allergies

Hakeem is built on InterSystems TrakCare with a centralised database. Within Hakeem-enabled facilities, the EHR-EP integration is mature: allergies, problems, and labs feed CDS in real time. Across non-Hakeem hospitals (most private chains), each facility has its own integration story; HL7 v2 is the dominant pattern, FHIR APIs are emerging. Cross-facility integration via the National Health Information Exchange is the next horizon.

Procurement question

When evaluating EP vendors, the most important integration question is not "do you support HL7?" — every vendor will say yes. Ask: "Show me a live FHIR endpoint exchanging allergies, labs, and meds with our existing EHR in our test environment." Demand evidence, not promises.

Extending EP into the community — a different beast

Hospital EP is a closed loop. Community e-prescribing is an open loop. The patient leaves the clinic with an electronic token and chooses any pharmacy. The architecture, the risks, and the regulation all change.

DimensionHospital EPCommunity e-Prescribing (eTP)

Figure 8 — The community e-prescribing flow. The "token" travels with the patient; the data travels via a national spine. Both must arrive at the same pharmacy.

Architecture insight

In community EP, the patient is the messenger of the token, but the data must arrive electronically through a trusted central spine. Without the spine, the system becomes a fancy printed prescription — losing CDS, audit, and pharmacovigilance benefits.

24 / 28   eTP models
▼

Models of community e-prescribing worldwide

Four archetypes dominate the landscape. Understanding them helps you place Jordan and design future-state services.

🇬🇧

NHS EPS (Electronic Prescription Service) — UK

GPs send prescriptions to a national spine. Patients nominate one pharmacy ahead of time, or the prescription token goes with them. Highly mature; most repeat prescriptions are paperless.

🇺🇸

SureScripts — USA

A private intermediary network connects EHRs to pharmacy chains. Volumes are enormous. Coverage of controlled substances (EPCS) is now standard in most states.

🇪🇺

Estonia / Finland / Denmark — National e-Prescription

Government-run, single-national-spine models with universal coverage. Patient picks up at any pharmacy with national ID. Cross-border pilots underway in EU.

🇯🇴

Hybrid / Emerging — Jordan, GCC

Public sector has Hakeem within facilities, but cross-organisational e-prescription to community pharmacies is largely paper. Insurance pre-authorisation and ChIP / claims systems are partly digital.

What every mature eTP model requires

  1. A single, trusted patient identifier across organisations (national ID, insurance number, or NHS-style number).
  2. A central spine or trusted exchange with high availability and strong audit logging.
  3. Authentication standards — digital signature for the prescriber, professional registration for the pharmacist.
  4. Controlled-drug protocols — special handling for narcotics, psychotropics, codeine combinations. Often a separate workflow with stronger authentication.
  5. A real-time medication record that both prescribers and dispensers can read (a "shared record" or PMR).
  6. Patient-facing access — a portal or app where patients can see their own prescriptions and choose pharmacies.
For Jordan

The technical pieces exist (Hakeem inside hospitals, the National ID, JFDA registration of pharmacies, insurance claims platforms). What is missing is the connecting tissue: a national e-prescribing standard, a controlled-drug e-prescription workflow, and a patient-facing portal across sectors. This is where your generation of pharmacy administrators will lead.

25 / 28   Hospital → Community
▼

The discharge transition — where systems most often fail

A patient leaving hospital is at peak medication risk: new drugs, stopped drugs, dose changes, and a handover to a primary-care team that may have no visibility of any of it.

What can go wrong

Discharge summary delays

Even when generated electronically, summaries can sit in batch queues for days before reaching the GP. Kirby et al. UK data: 80 days under paper, < 1 day with proper EP integration.

Medication reconciliation failures

Pre-admission medications inadvertently restarted; new drugs not communicated; dose changes lost in transit. National Patient Safety Agency data: medication errors at the hospital-community interface are one of the largest causes of preventable readmission.

Patient empty-handed

Patient discharged Friday afternoon with prescription that GP cannot fulfil over weekend. Patient runs out of medicines and presents to A&E on Monday.

Granularity loss

Hospital EP can capture reducing-dose regimens, alternate routes, IV-to-PO switches. Community systems often cannot receive this granular data — information is silently flattened in transit.

What a good discharge transition looks like

1
Med rec on admission

Pharmacist verifies pre-admission meds against patient/GP record

→
2
Inpatient EP

All changes captured in EP with rationale

→
3
Discharge med rec

Pharmacist reviews and reconciles before discharge

→
4
Real-time eDS

Discharge summary transmitted same-day to GP

→
5
Patient counselling

Pharmacist counsels patient with simplified med list

→
6
Community follow-up

Community pharmacist receives data, supports adherence

In Jordan today, the most reliable hospital-to-community handover is via the discharge prescription printed on hospital letterhead and physically given to the patient. The patient takes it to a community pharmacy of their choice. This works for simple cases. It fails for: complex tapering regimens, controlled drugs, oncology supportive care, and any patient with cognitive impairment. The opportunity cost of not having electronic discharge to community pharmacies is measurable in readmissions.

For your dissertation

The hospital-community medication transition is one of the highest-yield research topics for any Master in Pharmacy Administration thesis in Jordan today. There is published international literature; there is almost no Jordanian-context data. This is a gap waiting for you.

26 / 28   Jordan deep-dive
▼

Jordan's electronic prescribing landscape

A practical map for the working pharmacy administrator in Amman, Irbid, or Aqaba.

Public sector — the Hakeem ecosystem

🏛️ Electronic Health Solutions (EHS)

The national operator of Hakeem, the country's flagship EHR with EP and dispensing modules. Built on InterSystems TrakCare. Rollout includes MoH hospitals, RMS military hospitals, university hospitals, and primary-care comprehensive centres.

🎓 University Hospitals

Jordan University Hospital (JUH) and King Abdullah University Hospital (KAUH) have integrated Hakeem-based EP with their teaching, research, and pharmaco-epidemiology workflows.

🪖 Royal Medical Services (RMS)

Military health system covering active service personnel and their families. King Hussein Medical Center is the flagship; EP rollout closely tracks Hakeem.

🏛️ Ministry of Health (MoH)

Primary-care centres, district hospitals, specialised facilities. Adoption uneven; rural sites still partly paper. Antimicrobial stewardship and chronic-disease registries are key data outputs.

Private sector — heterogeneous and competitive

🏥 Major private hospitals

Istishari, Khalidi, Specialty, Jordan Hospital, Arab Medical Center — each runs its own EHR/EP (Cerner, TrakCare, locally built systems). Patient-facing portals and apps emerging. Limited cross-facility data exchange.

🦋 King Hussein Cancer Center (KHCC)

JCI-accredited oncology centre with bespoke chemotherapy EP, BCMA, and pharmacist-driven medication review. The Jordanian benchmark for closed-loop hospital EP.

💊 Community pharmacies

Approximately 3,000+ private community pharmacies. Dispensing software is common but rarely connected to a national prescribing spine. Most prescriptions still arrive on paper.

📋 Insurance & payers

Public (Civil Insurance, Royal Court, RMS), private (MedNet, GIG, NextCare, Globemed), and self-pay. Each with its own claims and pre-authorisation workflows. EP integration with payers is partial and varies by insurer.

Regulators and special programmes

  • Jordan Food & Drug Administration (JFDA) — drug registration, controlled-drug regulation, post-marketing surveillance, pharmacovigilance reporting.
  • National Center for Diabetes, Endocrinology and Genetics (NCD) — chronic-disease registries; potential anchor for population-level e-prescribing of insulin and oral hypoglycaemics.
  • Jordan Pharmacists Association (JPhA) — professional body; advocates for community-pharmacy integration with national e-prescribing.
  • Higher Health Council — strategic oversight of Jordan's national e-health strategy.

Where we stand: Hospital EP is mature within Hakeem-enabled facilities and at KHCC. Community e-prescribing is at an earlier stage — paper still dominates. Insurance pre-authorisation is partly digital. The next strategic horizon is a national e-prescription spine connecting GP, hospital, community pharmacy, and payer — with strong patient-facing access and controlled-drug protocols. The skills you build in this course (definitions, evidence appraisal, CDS design, governance) are exactly the skills that will design that horizon.

27 / 28   Take-home
▼

Take-home messages — five sociotechnical lessons

If you remember nothing else from these 90 minutes, remember these five sentences.

01

EP ≠ plug-and-play

EP systems require continuous monitoring, refinement, and adaptation to local context. Technology alone does not solve prescribing problems.

02

Design determines benefit

Poor design introduces new errors. User interface, workflow logic, and decision-support quality are the decisive factors in safety outcomes.

03

Culture matters as much as code

User engagement, training, and ownership determine whether EP systems are embraced or subverted through workarounds.

04

Integrate, don't isolate

EP works best when integrated with the EHR, the pharmacy system, and the discharge process — never as a standalone tool.

05

Monitor and adapt

Post-implementation surveillance is essential. Identify unintended consequences, track error patterns, and iterate system design.

★

The pharmacist is the integrator

Across hospital and community, the pharmacist is the only professional with both clinical knowledge and a daily stake in the medicines-data loop. EP succeeds when pharmacy leadership shapes it.

EP systems have immense potential to improve patient safety, streamline workflows, and enhance care quality. Realising that potential requires more than software — it demands thoughtful design, robust implementation, and ongoing commitment to improvement.
